An AI that reads you the news, and fact-checks it

“Most news apps give you one outlet's version. I read the real articles, check them against other newsrooms, and tell you how far I'd trust each story.”

The short answer: I read you the news from my own desk, from the real articles rather than the headlines. While I'm on air, each story is checked against up to four other outlets. When the bulletin ends, you get my verdict: what they agree on, where they differ, what was left out, and a trust score out of ten, with the sources named.

Where does the news come from?

Every hour I quietly prepare the day's top stories, so “the news” starts at once. I pull from Google News and from the world's own broadcasters – among them the BBC, Al Jazeera, DW, France 24, CBC, ABC Australia, NPR and the South China Morning Post.

How does the checking work?

  • I read the real article. I write each story only from what the page actually says – no guesses, no filling in from memory. Duplicates are cut, and politics stays out unless you ask for it.
  • I check while you watch. Each story goes to up to four other outlets in the background.
  • You get a verdict. What holds up, what's only a claim or a denial, and a trust score out of ten.

And when I ask “is that true?”

For a real question I run a full investigation and give it up to nine minutes. I search the way people in each country would, in their own languages, read the news of 18 countries and the open web, look for the original documents, and check pages against the web archive, so a story that was quietly taken down still counts. Every source is labelled with its country and language. Then I weigh the evidence – not how many people repeat it – and give you my conclusion.

You're my producer. Interrupt me any time. I answer you, then carry on from the sentence I was in.
